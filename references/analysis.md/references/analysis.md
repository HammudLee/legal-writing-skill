# Analysis and Organization

## 1. Map the Dispute Before Writing

Do not organize a discussion around individual cases. Organize it around the claims, defenses, or issues the law raises.

1. Identify every claim and defense potentially available.
2. Identify threshold questions: jurisdiction, justiciability, limitations, exhaustion, standing, or other issues that can end the analysis.
3. Break each claim into the legal requirements that control it. Those requirements may come from:
   - the elements stated in a case;
   - the text and structure of a statute;
   - a rule synthesized from several cases;
   - factors a court balances under a totality-of-circumstances test.
4. Put threshold issues first. Otherwise choose an order that is logical, easiest for the reader, or strategically strongest. If the chosen order differs from the natural order of a definition, explain why.
5. Within each issue, spend space where the real dispute is. Dispose of settled elements briefly; analyze contested elements in detail.

## 2. Read and Use a Case

Before citing a case as authority, identify:

- the parties, claims, procedural posture, and facts that the court treated as material;
- the issue the court decided;
- the holding, including its breadth and any limiting conditions;
- the reasoning connecting the facts and rule to the result;
- policy or fairness considerations;
- dicta, concurrences, dissents, and the level of court;
- subsequent history and whether the case remains good law.

When describing a case in prose, include enough facts, holding, and reasoning to show why the case supports the proposition. Start with the legal point the case illustrates, not with the party names or case history.

## 3. State and Synthesize the Rule

Build the rule from the strongest available authority:

1. Binding authority in the controlling jurisdiction first.
2. Lower court decisions only to the extent they apply binding law or are otherwise persuasive.
3. Persuasive authority from other jurisdictions when the forum has no controlling answer.
4. Secondary sources for explanation, not as a substitute for primary authority.

When more than one case contributes to the rule:

- Synthesize the cases into a general rule, with exceptions and factors visible.
- Do not present an unrelated list of case summaries.
- Show how the holdings fit together or conflict.
- When authority is split, identify the lines of cases, the jurisdictions or courts in each line, and why one line is more persuasive.

Distinguish holding from dicta. Do not treat a statement the court did not need to decide as controlling. When a case is distinguishable, explain the legally significant difference rather than merely saying the case is different.

## 4. Analyze Statutes

For statutory problems:

1. Quote or accurately paraphrase the operative text. Use the statute's defined terms.
2. Start with the text's ordinary meaning in context.
3. Read the provision with the surrounding statutory scheme, definitions, exceptions, cross-references, and purpose provisions.
4. Use legislative history only after text and structure, and only for what it actually supports.
5. Apply canons of construction when they genuinely resolve or illuminate ambiguity. Do not stack conflicting canons without explaining the choice.
6. Explain which reading the user's client or position favors, and state the best contrary reading.

When the problem concerns the meaning of a statute rather than its application to facts, the analysis should focus on interpretation, precedent, and policy, not only on comparing case facts.

## 5. Small-Scale Analysis

For each contested issue, use a transparent sequence. The common label is IRAC, but the underlying order matters more than the acronym:

1. **Rule explanation.** State the rule and explain ambiguous terms, tests, elements, or factors. Quote the controlling formulation when its words matter.
2. **Authority in action.** Show how prior decisions applied or developed the rule. Give the legally relevant facts, holding, and reasoning for each important case.
3. **Application.** Apply the rule to the current facts. Compare the facts with the precedents and explain why the analogies or distinctions control.
4. **Counteranalysis.** Address exceptions, adverse cases, and credible contrary interpretations. In objective writing, evaluate them; in advocacy, rebut them.
5. **Conclusion.** State the result for that issue and connect it to the overall claim.

This is a reasoning sequence, not a required five-paragraph template. A simple issue can be handled in one paragraph; a complex issue may need several paragraphs for one step. The rule and precedent discussion must precede the application because the reader needs the legal frame before the current facts have legal meaning.

## 6. Thesis Paragraph

For a memo Discussion or a substantial analysis, open with a thesis paragraph that tells the reader:

- the claim or claims analyzed;
- the controlling rule in short form;
- the main conclusion; and
- the issues or roadmap the discussion will follow.

Make the thesis proportional to the discussion. Do not bury the conclusion until the end of an internal legal analysis. A legal document does not build suspense; it states the answer and then justifies it.

## 7. Questions of Law and Argument Types

When the dispute is about what the law means, is missing, conflicts, or should change, choose and order the strongest arguments:

- plain meaning of the enacted text;
- statutory context and structure;
- legislative history and purpose;
- binding or persuasive precedent;
- broad or narrow framing of a holding;
- analogy to extend a rule or distinction to avoid one;
- competing lines of authority;
- overruling or refining an outdated rule;
- policy arguments such as fairness, deterrence, administrability, institutional competence, reliance, and judicial economy.

Authority arguments generally precede policy arguments, and plain meaning precedes legislative history. Argue in the alternative when it is genuinely useful: identify the first theory, then what follows if the court rejects it. Do not make contradictory factual claims.

## 8. Objective Versus Persuasive Analysis

For objective documents:

- State the best case for each side.
- Do not suppress adverse authority or likely losing arguments.
- Predict and explain the outcome, including uncertainty.
- Separate the writer's judgment from unsupported assertion.

For persuasive documents:

- Lead with affirmative reasons the client should win.
- Use assertive topic sentences and point headings.
- Anticipate and rebut the strongest contrary arguments.
- Do not call weak cases "dispositive" or overstate the facts.

Both modes require accurate authority, honest characterization of cases, and application to the actual facts.
