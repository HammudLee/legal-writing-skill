# Document Conventions

Use the user's assignment instructions, governing rules, and local court rules first. The structures below are defaults, not universal requirements.

## 1. Office Memorandum

An office memorandum is an internal, objective analysis for a lawyer or supervisor.

Typical components:

1. **Heading.** To, From, Date, and Re.
2. **Question Presented.** State the specific legal question and the material facts that make it live. Use "whether" or an interrogative form. Keep each question parallel and self-contained.
3. **Short Answer.** Answer the question directly in one or a few sentences, followed by the controlling reasons at a high level.
4. **Statement of Facts.** Give the legally significant facts in a clear order, usually chronological, topical, or chronological within topics. Include procedural facts and exclude irrelevant facts. Do not argue or analyze in this section.
5. **Discussion.** Open with a thesis paragraph and roadmap, then analyze each claim and issue using the sequence in `analysis.md`. Address adverse authority and uncertainty.
6. **Conclusion.** Summarize the result and its principal reasons when the format calls for it.

Writing notes:

- The memo is objective, formal, and internal. The reader is a lawyer; do not teach elementary procedure, but do supply the facts, holdings, reasoning, and application of cited authority.
- Avoid "I believe" or "I think" in the analysis. State what the law and facts support.
- Write sections in the order the reader will read them, but it is normal to draft the Discussion first and rewrite the front matter afterward.

## 2. Opinion Letter to a Client

An opinion letter explains the law and gives advice to a non-lawyer audience.

Typical components:

1. Opening that identifies the matter, the question asked, and the conclusion.
2. Relevant facts as the client would recognize them.
3. A concise explanation of the controlling rule in plain language.
4. The application of the rule to the client's situation, including strengths and risks.
5. The recommendation, options, next steps, and any deadlines.
6. Closing or signature block appropriate to the relationship.

Writing notes:

- Use plain English and define legal terms in context. Keep substantive legal terms, but explain them.
- Be direct about favorable and unfavorable possibilities. Do not promise a result.
- State assumptions and any facts that remain unverified.
- Preserve confidentiality and professional responsibility limits; this skill does not establish or simulate an attorney-client relationship.

## 3. Trial Motion and Memorandum of Law

A motion asks a court for a ruling. A memorandum of law supports or opposes it.

Typical components:

1. **Caption and title.** Court, parties, docket or index number, and document title.
2. **Introduction.** Identify the parties, nature of the case, motion, relief sought, and the basic reason for granting or denying it.
3. **Statement of facts or background.** Include procedural history and record facts needed to decide the motion.
4. **Question presented**, when the format calls for it.
5. **Standard.** State the governing standard, such as the rule for dismissal or summary judgment.
6. **Argument.** Use persuasive point headings; apply the standard and controlling authority to the record.
7. **Conclusion and relief requested.** State the precise order the court should enter.
8. Supporting materials such as affidavits or exhibits.

Motion-specific checks:

- A motion to dismiss usually takes the complaint's well-pleaded facts as true and tests legal sufficiency.
- A summary-judgment memo must show, using record citations, whether material facts are disputed and why judgment follows as a matter of law.
- Cite the record precisely for factual assertions: pleading paragraphs, depositions, affidavits, declarations, or ECF numbers.
- Affidavits and declarations state admissible facts from personal knowledge, not legal conclusions or argument.
- Check local rules for caption, page limits, hearing requirements, certification, and filing procedures.

## 4. Appellate Brief

An appellate brief asks an appellate court to affirm, reverse, or otherwise decide based on the record below.

Typical components:

1. **Title page and cover information.**
2. **Table of Contents**, including point headings and subheadings.
3. **Table of Authorities**, grouping cases, constitutional and statutory provisions, regulations, and other authorities.
4. **Jurisdictional statement or statement of the case**, where required.
5. **Question(s) Presented.** Include the legal basis and the key facts; write each question in the same grammatical form and in argument order.
6. **Statement of the Case.** Give a neutral but strategically framed procedural history and material facts, citing the record.
7. **Summary of the Argument.** State the theory of the case, controlling law, and result in an affirmative summary. Cite statutory language and crucial authority; avoid a list of topics.
8. **Argument.** Organize by issues, each introduced by a conclusive point heading. State the standard of review. Argue the strongest points first, subject to threshold and logical order.
9. **Conclusion.** State the precise relief requested.

Strategy checks:

- Select a small number of genuinely arguable issues. A diffuse brief weakens the strongest point.
- Identify the standard of review: de novo for questions of law, deferential for factual findings, abuse of discretion for discretionary rulings, and explain mixed questions.
- Put threshold and dependent issues in logical order. Otherwise balance the most persuasive issue against the most consequential relief.
- Do not retry facts on appeal. Use the record and demonstrate legal error.
- Point headings should be full assertions favorable to the client, not neutral topic labels. When read together, they should outline the argument.
- Avoid contradictory positions; alternative arguments may be proper when one concedes a theory and offers another.

## 5. Case Brief

For a study aid or preparatory brief, state concisely:

- citation and court;
- procedural posture;
- legally relevant facts;
- issue or issues;
- holding and the rule applied;
- reasoning;
- concurring or dissenting views and any important dicta;
- the case's significance for the current problem.

Write the brief in the user's words, except when quoting a formulation whose exact wording matters. The brief is a working tool, so clarity and precision matter more than complete coverage of every fact.

## 6. Final Document Check

Before delivering:

- Reconcile the Question Presented, roadmap, point headings, argument order, and conclusion.
- Confirm that every material fact appears in the facts section or argument where it belongs.
- Confirm that adverse authority is handled in objective writing and rebutted in advocacy.
- Confirm that the requested relief and jurisdictional or procedural posture are accurate.
- Verify page limits, font, margins, caption, signature blocks, and any user or court rules.
