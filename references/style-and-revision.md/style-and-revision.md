# Style and Revision

## 1. Writing Process

Use a recursive process, not a single polished draft:

1. Read the assignment and identify the audience, purpose, and constraints.
2. Read the authorities once for orientation.
3. Read them again for structure: issues, holdings, rules, reasoning, and distinctions.
4. Convert notes into an outline organized by claims and issues.
5. Draft the analysis before locking the introduction, question presented, or summary.
6. Revise first for organization and legal analysis, then for sentence and paragraph clarity.
7. Proofread last, including quotations, citations, headings, page limits, and document order.

Legal writing states the conclusion early and justifies it. Do not save the main result for a closing reveal.

## 2. Paragraphs

Each paragraph should develop one dominant idea.

- **Unity.** Every sentence should support the paragraph's topic.
- **Coherence.** Show the logical relationship between sentences and paragraphs.
- **Topic sentences.** Begin with the legal point or conclusion the paragraph develops. Do not begin with a case name, a citation, a fact dump, or a sentence that merely announces that a court "addressed" a topic.
- **Transitions.** Use transitions to connect precedents, move from rule to application, or shift to counterargument. Avoid mechanical connectors when the underlying relationship is unclear.
- **Length.** A paragraph running roughly a page or more often needs a logical subdivision. A very short paragraph should be deliberate, not an accidental fragment.

When checking unity, ask whether the topic sentence can be written in one sentence. If not, split or sharpen the paragraph.

## 3. Sentence Principles

Prefer:

- short, concrete subjects;
- verbs that carry the action;
- active voice, especially when it reveals who acted;
- subject and verb close together;
- short sentences with parallel structure;
- concrete, familiar, and specific words;
- precise legal terms when they are necessary to the analysis.

Avoid or fix:

- nominalizations such as "made a determination" for "determined";
- throat-clearing phrases such as "It is important to note that";
- misplaced and dangling modifiers;
- unclear restrictive or nonrestrictive modifiers;
- apostrophes on possessive pronouns;
- unnecessary adjectives, adverbs, qualifiers, and intensifiers;
- vague comparisons such as "more important" without the standard of comparison;
- words that do not have a rational relationship to the rest of the sentence;
- informal language, slang, and contractions in formal documents;
- sexist or unnecessarily exclusionary language.

Do not convert every sentence mechanically. Preserve nuance when a short sentence would overstate the law or hide a necessary qualification.

## 4. Audience and Diction

- For lawyers: use precise legal vocabulary and formal but direct English. Do not overexplain basic legal concepts, but do explain the facts, holding, reasoning, and application of the authorities.
- For clients: use plain language, explain legal terms, state risks clearly, and recommend concrete next steps.
- Avoid archaic legalese such as "herein," "hereinbefore," and "said document." Keep operative statutory or rule language and terms of art.
- Use the proper party voice in litigation documents. In an interoffice memo, the writer is the client's attorney, so "we filed" may be accurate; in a brief, use the party's designation or name and keep the tone professional.
- Do not insult opposing counsel, parties, or the court. Advocacy is stronger when it attacks the weakness of the argument rather than the character of its author.

## 5. Quotations

Use quotations when the exact language matters:

- operative statutory language;
- a court's formulation of a rule or standard;
- language whose precise wording affects the analysis.

Otherwise paraphrase and cite. Integrate quotations into a grammatical sentence, identify the speaker, and explain why the quoted language matters. Use block quotations only when their length and importance justify the visual interruption.

## 6. Revision Checklist

### Analysis

- Does the document identify every necessary claim, defense, element, factor, or issue?
- Are threshold issues placed first?
- Is the controlling rule stated before it is applied?
- Does each important precedent include the material facts, holding, and reasoning?
- Are the current facts compared to the precedent facts with meaningful analogies and distinctions?
- Are adverse authority and counterarguments handled in the correct mode?
- Do conclusions follow from the stated reasons?

### Organization

- Does the roadmap match the actual headings and order?
- Are questions, point headings, and conclusions consistent?
- Does each paragraph have one clear function?
- Are facts separated from analysis where the document requires it?

### Language

- Are subjects concrete and verbs active where appropriate?
- Are sentences readable and varied without becoming loose?
- Are legal terms retained while general prose is simplified?
- Are quotations accurate and introduced?

### Citations and Format

- Is every proposition of law supported by a real, correctly cited authority?
- Are pinpoint cites, short forms, subsequent history, and parentheticals correct?
- Are the citation format, font, margins, page limit, and filing rules correct?
- Read the document aloud or line by line to catch missing words, wrong homonyms, and grammar that automated checks miss.
