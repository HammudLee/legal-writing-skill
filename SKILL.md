# Legal Writing

Help the user produce clear, well-reasoned U.S. legal writing. The core task is to connect authority to facts, keep the reasoning visible, and match the document's audience and function.

This skill synthesizes working methods from three sources:

- Dernbach et al., *A Practical Guide to Legal Writing and Legal Method*
- Shapo, Walter & Fajans, *Writing and Analysis in the Law*
- *The Bluebook: A Uniform System of Citation*, 21st ed.

Treat those books as writing methodology, not as a source of legal rules. Legal conclusions still require actual authority and the user's facts.

## Before Drafting

Confirm the controlling parameters before producing a full document:

1. **Task and audience.** Is the document objective or persuasive, internal or external, for a lawyer, client, trial court, or appellate court?
2. **Jurisdiction and forum.** Identify the controlling jurisdiction, procedural posture, and any assignment rules, local court rules, style guide, page limit, or filing format supplied by the user.
3. **Available facts and sources.** Separate facts already in the problem from facts that would need research or user input. Do not invent missing facts, cases, quotations, docket numbers, or holdings.
4. **Deliverable.** Draft new text, outline, revise existing text, or check a completed document? Preserve the user's requested scope and file format.

If jurisdiction or governing rules are missing, state the assumption or ask before writing conclusions that depend on it. Do not silently assume U.S. federal law when a state or foreign issue is possible.

## Core Workflow

Use this sequence for substantive legal writing. Adapt the order to the document, but keep the reasoning chain explicit.

1. **Map claims and issues.** Identify each claim, defense, or motion. Break claims into elements, factors, or statutory conditions. Put threshold issues such as jurisdiction or limitations first.
2. **State the controlling rule.** Use binding authority first, then persuasive authority. Distinguish holdings from dicta, and explain the source and scope of the rule.
3. **Show the authority's application.** For each important precedent, state the legally relevant facts, holding, and reasoning; do not merely string together case names.
4. **Apply the rule to the user's facts.** Compare and contrast the facts and reasoning, explaining which similarities and distinctions matter.
5. **Address counterarguments.** In objective writing, evaluate both sides and adverse authority. In advocacy, rebut the strongest anticipated points after first building the affirmative argument.
6. **Conclude, then organize.** State the conclusion and roadmap where the document's conventions require them. Order issues by logic, importance, or strategy as appropriate.
7. **Revise and cite.** Check analysis, paragraph structure, sentence clarity, and citations. Cite every assertion of law that requires authority and verify each citation against a real source.

## Guardrails

- Preserve the objective/persuasive distinction. An office memorandum evaluates likely outcomes and discloses adverse authority; a brief argues for one result and uses conclusive point headings.
- Do not fabricate citations, case names, quotations, statutory text, or facts. Mark uncertain law or missing authority clearly and ask the user to verify it.
- Use authority hierarchy correctly: binding authority controls; persuasive authority is support, not a substitute.
- Preserve operative statutory or rule language even when simplifying surrounding prose. Avoid legalese, but retain terms of art.
- Follow the user's course instructions, court rules, and jurisdiction-specific citation requirements over the general defaults in this skill.
- This skill is a writing and analysis aid, not legal advice and not a substitute for attorney review.
